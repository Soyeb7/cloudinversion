What is a cloud inversion?

How a sea of cloud forms, when to look for one, and how high you need to climb to see it.

Normally the air gets colder as you climb, by roughly 6 to 7°C for every kilometre. A temperature inversion is a layer where that flips and the air gets warmer with height. Warm air sits on top of cold air like a lid. If the cold air underneath is damp enough to form fog or low cloud, that cloud is trapped below the lid, and from a summit above it you look down on a flat, white sea.

The three ingredients

Every cloud inversion needs all three at the same time. Two out of three gives you something else.

A warm lid
A layer where temperature rises with height. A degree or so is a weak lid; 3°C or more is a strong one. Without it, cloud simply mixes upwards and you get an ordinary grey day or hill fog on the summits.
Saturated air beneath it
Humidity near 100% under the lid, or the air temperature falling to within about 1°C of the dew point. Without moisture, an inversion just gives you a clear, frosty valley.
Drier, clearer air above
Humidity should drop sharply above the lid, with little cloud higher up. Otherwise you stand above one layer of cloud and under another, and miss the sunrise.

Two kinds of cloud sea

Valley fog

On a clear, calm night the ground radiates heat away. Cold air drains down the slopes and pools on the valley floors, and if it’s damp enough it condenses into radiation fog. These pools are usually shallow, from a few tens of metres to a couple of hundred metres deep, and fill the valleys rather than the whole landscape. They are at their best around sunrise and normally burn off within a few hours, although the fog often lifts into a layer of low cloud first. In a study of small valleys in Shropshire, the valley was more than 1°C colder than the hilltop on about 45% of nights; strong pools (over 4°C) formed on about 12%.

Anticyclonic stratus

Under a big area of high pressure, slowly sinking air warms and caps a damp lower layer, and a sheet of low cloud can sit beneath the lid for days. This is the extensive sea of cloud seen from the Lake District fells, Scottish summits or the Swiss Alps, where the cloud top is often 500 to 1,500 m up. It breaks up when a weather system brings colder air or stronger winds aloft.

How high do you need to be?

You need to stand above the top of the cloud. Under an elevated lid the cloud top sits at about the base of the warm layer; with valley fog it sits inside the cold pool. Cloud tops undulate and tend to rise as the morning warms, so aim for a viewpoint at least 50 to 100 m above the forecast top. If your summit is below the top, you’ll be standing inside the fog.

Staying safe and legal

Seeing a cloud inversion usually means walking uphill in the dark on a cold morning, sometimes through fog. Plan it like any other hill day, and be ready to turn back.

  • Use public rights of way and open access land, and follow local laws and access codes, such as the Countryside Code in England and Wales and the Scottish Outdoor Access Code in Scotland. Don’t cross private land, respect closures and seasonal restrictions, and park considerately.
  • Check the mountain forecast as well as this one. Wind, ice and fog on the hill can be very different from the valley.
  • Pick a route you know or have checked in daylight, and allow extra time in the dark.
  • Take a headtorch with spare batteries, a map and compass or offline map, warm layers, gloves and something to eat and drink.
  • Tell someone where you’re going and when you’ll be back.
  • Stay back from cliff edges, especially when cloud hides the drop.
  • In an emergency in the UK, call 999 and ask for the Police, then Mountain Rescue.

How this forecast works

  1. We fetch hourly forecasts from Open-Meteo, including temperature, humidity, wind and cloud at eight pressure levels from near the ground up to about 3 km, each with its real height.
  2. For each hour from about an hour before to two hours after sunrise we build a temperature profile from the model’s ground level upwards, ignoring levels that sit below the ground.
  3. We find the strongest layer where temperature rises with height (the lid) and measure how much it warms.
  4. We look for saturated air underneath it (model low cloud, humidity, dew-point spread and visibility) and estimate the cloud top from where the humidity drops away.
  5. We check for dry air and little cloud above, light winds and no rain. The lid and the moisture multiply together because both are essential; the other factors scale the result.
  6. Overnight cloud, pressure and its trend, recent rain, night length and how far your valley sits below the model’s smoothed terrain nudge the score up or down.

The result is a score out of 100, shown as Unlikely, Possible, Likely or Very likely. It’s a guide, not a probability: it hasn’t been calibrated against a long record of observed inversions.

Limits

  • Weather models smooth the terrain over a few kilometres, so deep, narrow valleys are shallower in the model and their fog is often missed.
  • Pressure levels near the ground are 200 to 300 m apart, so shallow fog and exact cloud-top heights are approximate.
  • Fog is one of the hardest things to forecast. Even professional fog rules have high false-alarm rates.
  • Accuracy drops quickly beyond a day or two. Check again the evening before.

Cloud inversion questions

Quick answers on what causes a sea of cloud and how to see one.

What is a cloud inversion?

A cloud inversion is a sea of cloud seen from above. It forms when a layer of warmer air sits on top of colder, damp air near the ground. The warm layer acts as a lid, trapping fog or low cloud in the valleys, so anyone on a hill above the lid looks down on a flat, white cloud sea in clear sunshine.

Is a temperature inversion the same as a cloud inversion?

Not quite. A temperature inversion is the warm layer itself: air that gets warmer with height instead of colder. A cloud inversion is what you see when there is also enough moisture underneath for fog or cloud to form, with clear air above. A temperature inversion without moisture just gives a clear, frosty valley.

What weather conditions cause a cloud inversion?

The best set-up is high pressure, light winds and a clear, long night after a damp day. The ground cools quickly under clear skies, cold air drains into the valleys and condenses into fog, and sinking air in the high pressure builds a warm lid above it. Strong winds, rain or a lot of high cloud usually stop an inversion forming.

What time of day is best to see a cloud inversion?

Around sunrise. Valley fog is usually deepest at dawn and burns off within a few hours as the sun warms the ground, often lifting into a layer of low cloud first. Under a strong winter high, a cloud inversion can last all day or even several days.

What time of year do cloud inversions happen?

Autumn and winter are best, roughly October to February in the UK. Long nights give the ground more time to cool and the low winter sun struggles to clear the fog. Radiation fog peaks in autumn, while long-lasting inversions under high pressure are most common in midwinter.

How high do I need to climb to be above a cloud inversion?

You need to be above the top of the cloud. Shallow valley fog can be just 50 to 200 metres deep, so a small hill is often enough. Inversions under high pressure usually have cloud tops between 500 and 1,500 metres. This checker estimates the cloud top for each morning and tells you how high to aim; add 100 metres or so for safety, because cloud tops ripple and rise as the morning warms.

Where are the best places to see a cloud inversion in the UK?

Places where hills rise above deep valleys or lakes: the Lake District (Latrigg, Cat Bells, Helvellyn), the Peak District (Mam Tor and Win Hill above the Hope Valley), Eryri (Moel Siabod, Yr Wyddfa), the Scottish Highlands (Ben Nevis, Conic Hill above Loch Lomond), Bannau Brycheiniog (the Sugar Loaf and Pen y Fan above the Usk valley) and the Yorkshire Dales.

Is it safe to climb a hill in the dark to see a cloud inversion?

It can be, with planning. Use public paths and open access land, follow local laws and access codes, and pick a route you know or have checked in daylight. Check the mountain forecast, tell someone your plans, take a headtorch, map and warm layers, keep away from cliff edges in fog, and turn back if conditions change. In an emergency in the UK, call 999 and ask for the Police, then Mountain Rescue.

What is a Brocken spectre?

A Brocken spectre is your own shadow cast onto cloud or fog below you, often surrounded by a rainbow-coloured ring called a glory. You need the sun low behind you and cloud in front of you, which makes a cloud inversion at sunrise one of the best times to see one.

How accurate is the cloud inversion forecast?

It is a guide based on weather model data, not a guarantee. Fog is one of the hardest things to forecast, and weather models smooth out narrow valleys, so the forecast is most reliable for broad, settled set-ups the evening before. Always check a local mountain forecast and conditions before heading out in the dark.
